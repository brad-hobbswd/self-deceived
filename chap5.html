Chapter 5 of 10

Conviction Without Change

SELF-DECEIVED · When Religious Knowledge Replaces Obedience

There is a difference between feeling bad about something and turning away from it. We may regret what happened, feel embarrassed, cry during prayer, or promise that we will never do the same thing again. Those feelings can be sincere. Yet repentance is more than being upset about the consequences. Repentance changes our direction.

Paul writes about godly sorrow and worldly sorrow in 2 Corinthians 7:10. One leads toward repentance; the other can leave us consumed by regret. We might be sorry that people found out, that a relationship became strained, or that our reputation took a hit. Godly sorrow goes deeper. It is willing to face the wrong itself and come back to God without excuses.

Picture a person who has spoken cruelly to a family member during an argument. Later, they send a message saying, 'I'm sorry you got upset. I was stressed, and you know how much I've been dealing with.' The message may sound like an apology, but the responsibility has been pushed back onto the other person. A genuine apology sounds different. It names what was done, recognizes the harm, and does not require the injured person to make the apology comfortable.

John the Baptist told people to produce fruit consistent with repentance (Matthew 3:8). Fruit is not a performance to earn forgiveness. It is evidence that the person is no longer content to remain in the same place. Sometimes the next step is making restitution. Sometimes it is correcting a lie, asking for help, ending a harmful pattern, or allowing someone to hold us accountable. Saying 'God knows my heart' cannot become a way of avoiding the practical change our heart needs to produce.

Accepting responsibility does not mean pretending circumstances had no effect. Pressure, exhaustion, fear, and painful experiences can help explain why a decision was difficult. They may deserve care and attention. Still, explanation cannot become a permanent excuse. We can acknowledge what influenced us and own what we chose. Both truths can be held honestly.

Promises are easy to make in the emotional aftermath of a mistake. Change often begins in quieter ways. We remove the opportunity that keeps leading us into trouble. We have the conversation we have been avoiding. We stop making excuses and seek wise support. We follow through even after the feelings have settled. Repentance is not proved by how dramatic the moment felt, but by the direction we begin taking afterward.

Growth does not always happen without setbacks. A struggle may require prayer, patience, accountability, and repeated effort. When we stumble, we should not hide the failure or pretend that nothing happened. We return to God, face what needs attention, and continue. Conviction is a mercy when it leads us toward change rather than simply making us feel guilty. The change God calls for will eventually reach the ordinary decisions of our day, and that is where obedience becomes visible.

Deceiving Your Own Heart

SELF-DECEIVED · When Religious Knowledge Replaces Obedience

Most of us do not wake up planning to deceive ourselves. We usually have a reason for what we did, and the reason sounds convincing when we tell it from our own side. We remember what happened before our reaction. We remember the pressure we were under. We remember what somebody else said. The trouble is that we can tell the story so carefully that our own responsibility disappears.

Jeremiah 17:9 warns us that the heart can be deceitful. That does not mean every feeling is false or that our experiences should be dismissed. Feelings can tell us something important. They simply cannot be the final judge of whether our conduct is right. When pride, fear, resentment, or personal advantage is involved, we need God's Word to help us see beyond our preferred version of events.

Consider a disagreement at work. A coworker questions the way you handled a task, and you feel embarrassed in front of others. You respond sharply. Later, when someone asks about the exchange, you explain that the coworker was disrespectful, that the timing was terrible, and that anybody would have reacted the same way. Those details may be true. Yet somewhere in the explanation, the question of your own words has been lost. You can acknowledge that another person behaved poorly without pretending your response was beyond correction.

Blame shifting is especially easy when somebody else really has done something wrong. We may focus on their conduct until we no longer examine ours. Scripture does not require us to accept false blame, excuse mistreatment, or ignore wrongdoing. Still, their responsibility does not erase ours. We can ask, 'What was mine to do? What did I say? What choice do I need to make differently?' Those questions are uncomfortable, but they are honest.

Comparison gives us another escape. We look at someone whose behavior appears worse and feel better about our own. Jesus addressed this in Matthew 7:3–5 when He spoke about noticing the speck in another person's eye while overlooking the plank in our own. He was not forbidding correction. He was challenging the blindness that makes us eager to examine others and reluctant to examine ourselves. The person we compare ourselves with cannot become the standard God gave us.

We also lean on our intentions. 'I was only trying to help.' 'I didn't mean to hurt anybody.' 'That's just how I am.' Good intentions may explain part of a situation, but they do not automatically make the outcome good. A comment meant as a joke can humiliate someone. Advice meant to help can become controlling. Honest concern can turn into gossip. We need enough humility to consider the effect of our actions, not only the story we tell about our motives.

Self-deception becomes stronger when every correction is treated as an attack. We may stop listening, change the subject, or collect reasons why the person speaking has no right to say anything. Sometimes the person correcting us may be wrong or unfair. We can examine that carefully without refusing every opportunity for truth. A teachable heart does not have to agree with every accusation, but it is willing to ask whether something needs to be corrected.

Paul tells believers to examine themselves in 2 Corinthians 13:5. This is not an invitation to live in constant suspicion of ourselves. It is a call to honesty. What facts am I leaving out? What would I say if I were not trying to protect my image? Am I asking others to practice something I excuse in myself? The heart can be very persuasive. We need a standard that does not bend to our excuses, and the next chapter turns us toward that standard.
